A

The thesis, and what was actually tested

A conventional recommender answers one question: what will this user like? It cannot say why it believes that, how much of its evidence comes from reviews that may be manipulated, or whether the user's taste has since moved on.

DyTrustRec treats a user–item interaction not as a fact but as evidence of varying reliability and varying age. Four claims were built into the design, and each was made falsifiable:

  1. Semantics help. Review text, encoded with SBERT, carries preference signal that IDs alone do not.
  2. Trust helps. An unsupervised graph anomaly detector (CoLA) can identify unreliable interactions, and down-weighting them improves ranking.
  3. Recency helps. Interest drifts, so recent interactions deserve more weight.
  4. A learned fusion of semantic and behavioural features beats plain concatenation.

Claim 1 held, more strongly than before. Claims 2, 3 and 4 were refuted — recency no longer helps even in combination with trust — and claim 2 failed in a particularly informative way: the trust signal turned out to be anti-correlated with the manipulation it was designed to catch. A fifth, unplanned result sits alongside them: weighting the training loss by that same (inverted) trust signal improved ranking substantially, which section Q reports without a settled explanation.

What is and is not novel here

HGT (Hu et al., 2020), SBERT (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019), CoLA (Liu et al., 2021), BPR (Rendle et al., 2009) and LightGCN (He et al., 2020) are all existing published work. None of them is a contribution of this project. The contribution is their integration into one pipeline, the specific trust transformation, and — above all — the experimental evaluation that determined whether any of it works.

B

Findings, stated up front

Every number below comes from a real run on the dev tier, seed 42. Nothing is projected or estimated.

Confirmed SBERT semantics improve ranking

Adding SBERT review embeddings to the plain HGT lifted NDCG@10 from 0.0133 to 0.0164 — +23.6%. This is the clearest positive effect measured, and the largest in the project.

Confirmed Graph models buy catalogue reach

HGT arms recommend across 23–34% of the catalogue against matrix factorisation's 7% and popularity's 0.2%, at markedly higher novelty. If the objective includes discovery rather than NDCG alone, that trade is real and measurable.

Refuted The trust component is worse than a coin flip

Against synthetically injected shill campaigns, CoLA-derived trust scored 0.2951 ROC AUC — below the 0.5 chance line. It ranks injected shills as more trustworthy than genuine users. A one-line rule counting duplicated review text scores 0.9895 on the same data.

Refuted Recency and trust do not help, even together

Trust alone cost −17.1% NDCG@10; recency alone was −4.8%; combined they cost −11.6%. The earlier reading — that they help only in combination — did not survive the sentiment-model swap (section J); with fresh features, neither the individual nor the combined effect is positive.

Refuted The learned fusion layer hurts

The learned Semantic-Behavioural Fusion Layer cost −27.7% NDCG@10 against plain concatenation. Not demonstrated to work on this data.

Unsettled The trust-weighted objective now helps, a lot

Weighting the BPR loss by the same CoLA trust scores that the panel above shows are inverted improved NDCG@10 by +43.8% over the fusion arm (0.0105 → 0.0151) — the opposite of what was found before the sentiment-model swap. It is the largest single delta in the ablation grid after SBERT. Section Q states this plainly rather than explaining it away; no mechanism for it is confirmed.

Refuted No graph arm beat matrix factorisation

Plain BPR-MF scored 0.0180 NDCG@10; the best HGT arm (B, SBERT only) reached 0.0164 — a clearer gap than before, not a narrower one.

Three findings that changed the design

1. No arm64 wheels exist for torch-scatter or torch-sparse. The project is therefore written not to need them — PyG's pure-Python layers cover everything used here.

2. But pyg-lib does have an arm64 macOS wheel for this exact torch build. Without it PyG raises ImportError: 'NeighborSampler' requires either 'pyg-lib' or 'torch-sparse' and mini-batch training on a heterogeneous graph is impossible. Installing it unlocked NeighborLoader.

3. Electronics does not fit. At 21.06 GB of reviews plus 4.89 GB of metadata against 30 GB free, the category the brief preferred cannot be materialised. That forced the three-tier design in section D.

Sampling, and declaring its bias

Three strategies are implemented, and each records what it does to representativeness in the run manifest:

  • head — first N records. Cheap, and biased: the files are not shuffled, so a prefix over-represents whoever appears early.
  • reservoir — uniform over interactions. Unbiased per interaction, but it fragments user histories, which distorts the recency signal.
  • user_core (default) — uniform over users, keeping every interaction of each sampled user. Preserves complete histories, which temporal modelling depends on.

A row cap is spent user-by-user: a user is either fully in or fully out. Truncating the record stream mid-user would hand later users artificially short histories — precisely the signal the temporal component reads.

The category discovery

main_category is 80% a single value ("Video Games") inside a single-category corpus — nearly useless as a node type. But categories is a root-to-leaf path, and its levels turned out to be orthogonal, not merely coarse-to-fine:

Platform and product type are different preference axes, so which belongs on the Category node is an empirical question. It became a config parameter (category_level: l1|l2|leaf, default L2) rather than a hardcoded guess.

The governing rule

A field that does not exist is reported as absent, never invented. The schema adapter returns an explicit availability report, and downstream modules degrade rather than fabricate. This is why the graph has five node types, not six.

Why suspicious rows are kept

The anomaly detector cannot find what the cleaner already removed. Deleting shill-like rows here would both destroy the evidence and flatter the trust component's later results. Five rule-based flags are attached — empty text, extreme length, text repeated across items, review bursts under 60 s, zero rating variance — each with a stated reason.

Disclosed, not hidden: k-core selection leakage

k-core filtering uses interaction counts over the whole timeline, including the test period. This is standard practice in the recommender-systems literature, but it is a mild form of selection leakage. It is recorded in every cleaning report and can be disabled in config.

I

Leakage control

Six specific guarantees, each asserted in the test suite rather than assumed:

  • max(train.timestamp) ≤ min(val.timestamp), and likewise val → test.
  • The training graph contains only training edges.
  • Scalers, item means and detectors are fitted on the training partition alone — enforced by a LeakageSafeScaler that records its fitted scope and refuses to transform before a fit.
  • Recency weighting is referenced to max(train.timestamp), never wall-clock now — otherwise the reference sits inside the test period and results depend on the day the script ran.
  • Negative sampling excludes positives from every split, not just train. Sampling only against train positives would place val/test items in the negative pool and silently inflate the metrics.
  • Interactions after the reference instant are clamped to age 0, so no row is ever up-weighted for being in the future.

The one deliberate exception, k-core selection, is documented in section F rather than quietly permitted.

J

Features

Semantic — SBERT

all-MiniLM-L6-v2, verified at 384 dimensions, max_seq_length 256, unit-normalised so dot product equals cosine. Review title and body are concatenated because Amazon titles routinely carry the verdict ("Broke after two weeks") the body only elaborates. Empty text receives a zero vector, not the embedding of the empty string, so "no text" stays representable.

115,342 reviews encoded in 394 s and cached to disk keyed by a digest of the model name, pooling settings and the texts themselves. A measured detail: on this M4, CPU beat MPS for short-text batches (0.06 s vs 0.81 s per 256 texts), because a 22M-parameter model never amortises MPS warm-up and transfer. device: auto therefore benchmarks both and picks the winner rather than reaching for the accelerator by reflex.

Structured — six per-review features

sentiment_compound (BERT — see below), review_length_log, helpful_votes_log, verified_purchase_f, recency_weight, rating_centred. The last two are leakage-sensitive and sit behind a fit/transform split: item means come from training rows only, and items unseen in training fall back to the global training mean, never to their own future mean.

The original choice here was VADER, deliberately: rule-based, so it needs no training data, cannot overfit this corpus, and introduces no opportunity for label leakage.

Regenerated 2026-09-09 src/features/sentiment.py has since been switched from VADER to nlptown/bert-base-multilingual-uncased-sentiment, a BERT model fine-tuned on Amazon/Yelp-style product reviews to predict a 1–5 star rating, remapped to the same pos/neg/neu/compound schema. It runs frozen (inference only), so the no-training-data-leakage property above still holds. Every number and figure in this report — sentiment_compound itself, and everything downstream of it (fusion, graph, HGT, the results in sections P and Q) — has been regenerated end to end under the BERT-based pipeline, same seed (42), same split, same training config apart from a training-mode fix noted in section V. Where the new numbers changed the finding, not just the decimal, section Q says so explicitly rather than quietly updating a table.

Fusion — the layer that did not work

A two-layer MLP over [sbert ‖ structured]: LayerNorm → Linear(390→256) → GELU → Dropout(0.2) → Linear(256→128) → LayerNorm. The input normalisation matters because the SBERT block is unit-norm while the structured block is standardised but unbounded; without it, 384 semantic dimensions dominate 6 behavioural ones purely by count.

Crucially, mode="concat" exists as an ablation arm so the question "does the learned projection beat plain concatenation?" could be answered rather than assumed. It was answered: no. See section Q.

There is no Seller node

Amazon Reviews 2023 has no seller field. Inventing one would put fabricated structure into the graph, so the architecture has five node types and the diagrams say so. brand is itself derived from store — usually but not always the manufacturer — and is labelled as a proxy wherever brand results appear.

Edge weighting

w_e = base · trust_e^α · temporal_e^β
temporal_e = exp(−λ · age_years),  λ = 0.5,  floor 0.01

Both factors default to 1.0 when disabled, so switching trust or recency off in an ablation leaves the plain graph exactly as it was rather than subtly rescaling every edge. Measured on the dev tier: mean weight 0.2412, range [0.0100, 1.0000].

L

CoLA and the trust transformation

CoLA's published formulation operates on homogeneous attributed graphs, so it cannot be pointed at a heterogeneous graph as-is. The adaptation is explicit and documented rather than glossed:

  1. Project the heterogeneous graph down to a user–product bipartite graph.
  2. Train contrastively: score each node by how well its own attributes can be predicted from an RWR-sampled local subgraph. 100 epochs, final loss 1.358.
  3. Score over 32 rounds — a single round is noisy. Output: mean −0.0285, range [−0.476, 0.257].
  4. Normalise by rank, chosen because it is invariant to the score distribution's shape. Min-max is dominated by a single extreme; a z-score sigmoid assumes a normality these scores do not have.
  5. Transform: trust = 1 − normalised_anomaly, clamped to [0.05, 1.0].

Why the floor is 0.05 and not 0

A trust of exactly 0 in a trust-weighted loss silently deletes an interaction from training and changes the effective dataset size — which would confound the very ablation it is supposed to inform.

Resulting distribution: mean 0.5013, std 0.2867. Three things this section is deliberate about: CoLA does not output trust (it outputs an unbounded anomaly score, and the conversion is a modelling choice); CoLA is not the recommender (it only reweights edges); and the whole chain is unsupervised, because this dataset has no fraud labels.

Type-specific linear projections map each node type's differently-sized features to 128 dimensions, then two HGT layers apply relation-aware multi-head attention. BPR was chosen as the primary objective because the signal is implicit, it optimises pairwise ranking (which is what the metrics measure), and it is the standard objective for LightGCN and MF — so every model is trained on like terms.

An MPS-specific fix

Installing pyg-lib for NeighborLoader routed HGTConv through pyg::segment_matmul, which has no MPS kernel. PYTORCH_ENABLE_MPS_FALLBACK does not help, because that only covers aten operators, not custom pyg ones. Fixed by setting torch_geometric.backend.use_segment_matmul = False on MPS — which disables only the fused typed-linear path and retains neighbour sampling.

N

Baselines

Four, all trained on the same split and scored by the same evaluator. Presenting HGT numbers without them would prove nothing.

  • Popularity — global training frequency. Not a strawman here: with a Gini of 0.521, recommending the head is genuinely strong.
  • Item-kNN CF — cosine similarity over the implicit matrix, top-50 neighbours. Item-based rather than user-based because 15,704 users against 6,619 items makes the item–item matrix both smaller and better estimated.
  • Matrix factorisation — BPR-MF, 64 dimensions, 50 epochs.
  • LightGCN — 64 dimensions, 3 propagation layers.
O

Evaluation protocol

Full-catalogue ranking — every model scores all 6,619 items for each evaluable user, with training positives masked to −inf. Conventions fixed once so every model is scored identically:

  • K is a cut-off; lists shorter than K are not padded.
  • A user with no held-out relevant item is excluded from the average rather than scored 0. Including them would penalise every model in proportion to a property of the split, not of the model.
  • NDCG uses binary relevance with ideal DCG over min(K, |relevant|).
  • Coverage and novelty are computed over the post-k-core catalogue, not the full Amazon catalogue, and are reported with that caveat.

Metrics: Precision@K, Recall@K, NDCG@K, MAP@K, MRR, Hit Rate, plus coverage, intra-list diversity and novelty (mean self-information, −log₂ p(item)).

They moved enough to overturn two of the readings below the original run reported.

Reading it honestly

SBERT is the one component that clearly earns its place. +23.6% is the largest positive effect measured anywhere in the project, and larger than it was under VADER-derived sentiment.

Trust and recency no longer help, even together. Trust alone costs 17.1%; recency alone costs 4.8%; combined they cost 11.6%. Under the previous sentiment feature this pair combined to a +8.4% gain — that result did not survive the swap. The direction of every individual arm (trust hurts, recency is roughly neutral-to-negative) stayed the same; only the combination flipped from a net positive to a net negative.

The fusion layer is refuted; the trust-weighted objective is not. The learned fusion layer lost to plain concatenation by 27.7%, consistent with the earlier run. But the trust-weighted training objective — weighting BPR loss by the same CoLA trust scores section R shows are inverted — gained 43.8% on top of it, reversing the earlier −19.3% finding. No mechanism for this reversal is confirmed here: it may be that up-weighting the (mislabelled) "high-trust" edges incidentally concentrates gradient on the interactions the fusion layer under-fits, or it may be single-seed variance on top of an already-weak F arm (0.0105 NDCG@10) — the report's own single-seed caveat (section V) applies with particular force to a result this size. It is reported as measured, not explained away.

R

Why the trust component failed

The corpus has no fraud labels, so trust was tested against synthetically injected shill campaigns where labels exist by construction: 785 shill users, 11,775 injected rows, 78 target items, sharing templates, a forced 5.0 rating and 30-minute burst windows.

CoLA is the only detector that falls to the left of the chance line. It is not failing to find the shills — it is actively preferring them.

The mechanism

CoLA's contrastive objective scores a node by how well its own attributes can be predicted from its neighbourhood context. A coordinated campaign is internally consistent by construction: the injected users share templates, share a forced rating, and overlap on target items, so each one is unusually easy to predict from the others.

A detector that equates "anomalous" with "unpredictable from context" will therefore rate a large, well-organised campaign as exemplary. Isolated eccentric users look stranger to it than a botnet does.

This was re-checked against the raw anomaly score with the trust transform bypassed, and the direction is unchanged — so it is a property of the detector on this data, not a sign error in the mapping.

The two negative results are one finding — but not the whole story

Trust weighting alone cost 17.1% NDCG@10. If trust is anti-correlated with manipulation, then trust weighting up-weights the manipulated edges and down-weights genuine ones — exactly the direction of harm observed in the ablation. The section Q result and the section R result explain each other rather than being independent failures.

That story does not, however, extend to loss-weighting by the same trust scores (ablation G), which now helps by 43.8% (section Q). If the trust scores are genuinely inverted, weighting the objective by them should hurt for the same reason edge-weighting does — it does not, and this document does not resolve why. Read as a caution against the tidy version of this finding, not as evidence the inversion above is wrong: the inversion is re-checked directly against the raw anomaly score, above.

What would have to change

Three candidates, none tested here, offered as the honest next experiments rather than as claims:

  • The projection. A user–user co-occurrence graph would reward dense coordinated cliques rather than treating them as ordinary neighbourhoods.
  • The detector. A density- or reconstruction-based objective, rather than a contrastive-predictability one, would not have this inversion built in.
  • The target. Score the interaction rather than the user.

And the blunt comparison worth keeping in view: a one-line rule counting a user's duplicated review text reached 0.9895 AUC. On this generative process, the entire graph-anomaly pipeline is beaten by a single groupby.

S

Explanations

Explanations are assembled from retrieved graph evidence only. Six sources are searched — shared category, shared brand, similar high-trust users, recent interactions, semantic similarity, review sentiment — and a template with no supporting evidence is dropped, never emitted with invented content.

A live example from the running system, for a user with 180 training interactions and trust 0.689:

'Grand Theft Auto V: Premium Edition - Xbox One [Digital Code]'
was recommended because 256 high-trust users with overlapping
history also bought it, the user's recent activity is
concentrated in 'xbox one', and supporting reviews are
strongly positive.

When nothing is found, the system says so explicitly rather than producing plausible filler — a behaviour pinned by a test asserting that an unknown user yields has_support == False and the phrase "no graph evidence".

T

Counterfactuals — the "why not?" module

For an item ranked below the top-K, a bisection search finds the smallest change to one perturbable factor — category affinity, brand affinity, recency weight, trust weight, semantic similarity — that would lift it across the boundary.

Three outcomes are possible, and all three are honest: the item is already in the top-K (reported as such); a perturbation is found (reported with the required magnitude and the achieved rank); or no tested factor within bounds moves it (reported as infeasible). The third case is the one that matters — the module never manufactures a plausible-sounding reason when the search fails.

U

Confidence

A ranking score is not a confidence, and the two are computed separately. Confidence blends three components: predictive stability under Monte Carlo dropout (0.5), evidence volume from interaction count (0.3), and evidence trust from supporting neighbours (0.2).

The behaviour that makes it worth having is the cold-start case: a user with one interaction must come out with low confidence even when the score is high. That is asserted directly in the test suite, along with the requirement that the stated reason names the penalty.

V

Defects found by running, not by reading

Six real bugs surfaced only when the code met real data. Each is listed with its symptom, cause and fix.

1 · user_core truncated histories

The row cap was applied by cutting the record stream part-way through, so users late in the file kept partial histories — destroying the exact property user_core exists to provide, and corrupting the recency signal. Fix: the cap is spent user-by-user; a user is fully in or fully out.

2 · k-core shipped a violated invariant

A 10-pass ceiling with only a warning on failure, so a non-converged run silently returned data breaking the k-core guarantee — which is what happened on the first smoke run. Fix: raises on non-convergence, ceiling 100, post-condition asserted before returning. The dev tier needs exactly 10 passes, so the original ceiling sat on the edge.

3 · max_products was dead config

Declared in YAML and read by nothing. Fix: a reported guard recorded in the run manifest, which deliberately does not truncate — dropping items by popularity would bias the coverage and novelty metrics, and dropping users would undo fix 1.

4 · A configured feature was silently dropped

fusion.structured_features named verified_purchase but the builder produces verified_purchase_f, so review nodes were built at 389-d instead of 390-d — the model trained on a narrower input than the config claimed. Fix: the mismatch is now fatal, not a warning.

5 · HGT crashed on MPS

Installing pyg-lib routed HGTConv through pyg::segment_matmul, which has no MPS kernel. PYTORCH_ENABLE_MPS_FALLBACK does not help because it covers aten operators, not custom pyg ones. Fix: use_segment_matmul = False on MPS, retaining neighbour sampling.

6 · LightGCN never converged

BPR loss pinned at exactly 0.69315 = ln(2) — positive and negative scores identical. Diagnosed: layer-averaging shrinks embedding norms until dot products are ~1e-3, sigmoid sits at 0.5 and gradients vanish. Measured: at lr=1e-3 the item-embedding norm decays to 0.04; at lr=0.05 with no weight decay it reaches 4.27 and trains. NDCG@10 improved 0.0047 → 0.0075.

And one excuse that was tested and eliminated

The obvious defence of HGT's underperformance is that it trains full-graph at ~100 optimiser steps while MF and LightGCN get 750–1500 mini-batch steps. A mini-batch rerun via LinkNeighborLoader was launched to equalise the budget. Arm A reached 684 steps and scored NDCG@10 0.0087, against 0.0141 at 100 full-batch steps (both figures from the pre-sentiment-swap run) — 6.8× more steps, materially worse. Neighbour sampling adds gradient noise that 12 epochs does not overcome. At ~3 hours per arm the remaining six were not run.

The training budget is not why HGT trails matrix factorisation here. (Partial evidence: one arm, one seed; a longer mini-batch schedule remains untested.)

Not repeated after the sentiment-model swap: at ~3 hours for one arm alone (~21 for all seven), re-verifying a training-dynamics finding that has nothing to do with which sentiment feature is used was not worth the machine-time. The underlying training.full_batch flag was, in fact, left at false from this exact experiment when this project's sentiment backend was swapped — the graph in section P was briefly, accidentally trained in the slow, memory-heavy mini-batch mode this panel already found worse, before being reset to full_batch: true.

W

Limitations

  1. Single seed. Three seeds are configured but were not run. No confidence intervals, so small deltas are not distinguishable from noise — including the F→G +43.8% swing in section Q, the largest single-seed-sensitive result in this report.
  2. Only 1,862 of 17,223 users are evaluable. The strict temporal split leaves 42.3% of test rows with an unseen user and 59.7% with an unseen item. The evaluation is noisy and biased toward users active late in the timeline.
  3. Trust is validated only synthetically — and fails. Whether the failure transfers to real Amazon manipulation is untestable here, because no labels exist.
  4. This is not a temporal GNN. Despite the "Dynamic" in the name, there is no TGN, TGAT or temporal attention — it is exponential recency weighting plus preference snapshots, and it is described that way everywhere.
  5. k-core selection leakage is present by design and disclosed in every cleaning report.
  6. Brand is a proxy for store; there is no seller modelling because the field does not exist.
  7. Coverage and novelty are catalogue-relative, computed over the post-k-core catalogue.
  8. CoLA is adapted, not applied as published — it runs on a bipartite projection.
  9. Reproducible up to backend non-determinism. MPS offers no deterministic-algorithm guarantee, so bit-exactness across runs is not claimed.

Z

The questions you will actually be asked

"What is your actual novelty?"

Not HGT, SBERT or CoLA — all existing published work. The contribution is their integration into one explainable pipeline, the specific anomaly-to-trust transformation, and the experimental evaluation that determined whether any of it works. That evaluation returned a largely negative answer, which is itself the result.

"Does CoLA detect fake reviews?"

No. On this project's own synthetic benchmark it scores 0.2951 ROC AUC — below the 0.5 chance line — meaning it ranks injected shills as more trustworthy than genuine users. A one-line duplicate-text rule scores 0.9895 on the same data. The mechanism is in section R: CoLA rewards predictability-from-context, and a coordinated campaign is internally consistent by construction.

"Why does your system lose to matrix factorisation?"

On NDCG@10 it does — the best HGT arm reaches 0.0164 against MF's 0.0180, a real if modest gap even allowing for single-seed noise. The obvious excuse (training budget) was tested and eliminated on the earlier VADER-based run: 6.8× more optimiser steps made it worse (see section V for that caveat). What the graph models do win on is catalogue reach, 23–34% against 7%. If the deployment objective is discovery rather than head-item accuracy, that is the relevant axis.

"Is this a failed project?"

The pipeline works, is reproducible, is covered by 229 tests, and is honestly measured. What failed is a hypothesis — that a contrastive graph-anomaly detector yields a useful trust signal for recommendation. Measuring that cleanly, and being able to explain the mechanism of the failure, is a result. Reporting it as a success would not have survived this document.

"What would you do next?"

In order: run the three configured seeds so the deltas acquire error bars; replace the CoLA projection with a user–user co-occurrence graph that rewards dense coordinated cliques; and test a longer mini-batch schedule, since 12 epochs was clearly too few and 100+ was never tried.
